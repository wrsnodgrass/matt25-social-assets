If you are twelve to eighteen months from retirement, you are in the most financially consequential window of your entire working life. The decisions you make in this stretch are going to shape your income, your taxes, and your legacy for the next thirty years. Most people spend more time planning a vacation than planning this.

These are the six decisions you cannot afford to get wrong in your last year before retirement.

Quick note before I get into it: I'm going to use round numbers and examples to make these concrete. Your numbers will look different. Talk to your own advisor before you act on any of this.

Decision one. When to claim Social Security.

This is the one everyone has an opinion on, and most of those opinions are wrong. Every year you delay past your full retirement age, your benefit grows by roughly eight percent. That is a guaranteed return you cannot get anywhere else. But waiting is not always the right call. Your health, your spouse's benefit, whether you actually need the income to cover your expenses in the early years, all of it changes the math. What I see most often is people claiming early because they don't have a clear income plan, so they default to taking the money as soon as they can get it. That is a reactive decision, not a strategic one. Run the numbers before you claim.

Decision two. How much to convert before your paycheck stops.

This one is counterintuitive. The year before you retire, and the first few years after, are often the lowest-income years you will have had since early in your career. If you have a traditional IRA or 401k, those years are potentially the cheapest time you will ever pay tax on that money. Converting some of it to a Roth now, before Required Minimum Distributions force you into a higher bracket at 73, is a tax strategy worth running the numbers on. It is not about finding a shortcut. It is about choosing when and at what rate you pay what you already owe.

Decision three. How you are going to bridge healthcare before Medicare.

Medicare starts at 65. If you retire before that, you have a gap. COBRA, marketplace coverage, a spouse's plan, these have very different costs, and the wrong choice can run thousands of dollars a year you didn't budget for. This is the one that catches people completely off guard because they assume their employer coverage just continues. It doesn't. You need a plan for this before you hand in your badge, not after.

Decision four. Building your income floor.

Your income floor is the amount of guaranteed monthly income that covers your non-negotiable expenses. Housing, food, utilities, insurance. Social Security covers part of it. A pension covers part of it if you have one. Beyond that, some people use an annuity, some use a bond ladder. The strategy depends on your situation, but the principle is the same: guaranteed income covering your fixed costs before you touch your investment portfolio. That way, a bad market year is uncomfortable but not catastrophic.

Decision five. When in the year you actually retire.

Almost nobody talks about this one. If you retire in October and the market drops fifteen percent by December, you are selling investments to fund your income at the worst possible moment. Sequence of returns risk is most dangerous in the first two years of retirement. When you retire within the calendar year, what your cash reserves look like on day one, and how much buffer you have to avoid selling equities at a loss, these details matter more than most people realize until it is too late to do anything about them.

Decision six. Whether you have a written plan.

I saved this one for last because it ties the other five together. Most people heading into retirement have a general idea. They know their Social Security estimate, they know roughly what's in their accounts, they have a date in mind. A general idea is not a plan. A plan has a Social Security strategy, a tax strategy, a healthcare bridge, an income floor, a sequence buffer, and a document that puts all of it in one place so you know what you are doing and why. Without that, you are making the biggest financial decisions of your life by feel.

Proverbs 21:5 says the plans of the diligent lead to profit. This is what that looks like in practice. Not rushing into it. Not guessing. Not defaulting to whatever seems easiest in the moment. Running the numbers, making these six decisions on purpose, before the window closes.
