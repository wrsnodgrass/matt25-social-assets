If you are twelve to eighteen months away from retirement, you are in the most financially consequential window of your entire working life. The decisions you make in this stretch, not the ones you made at 40, not the ones you'll make at 75, but the decisions you make right now, are going to shape your income, your taxes, and your legacy for the next thirty years.

Most people spend more time planning a two-week vacation than they spend planning this.

And I get it. Retirement feels like it's still in the future. You're still busy. You're still working. But the problem with waiting until you are actually retired to make these decisions is that by then, several of the best options you had are gone. The window closes. And you live with that for the rest of your life.

So today I want to walk you through the six decisions that determine whether your retirement is everything you worked for, or whether you spend the first ten years of it trying to undo mistakes you made in the last twelve months of your career.

Quick note before I get into it: I'm going to use round numbers and examples to make this concrete. Your numbers will look different. Talk to your own advisor before you act on any of this.

Let's start with decision one. When to claim Social Security.

This is the one everyone has an opinion on, and most of those opinions are based on one piece of information that they heard from someone at a dinner party. So let me give you the actual math.

Every year you delay claiming past your full retirement age, your benefit grows by roughly eight percent. That is a guaranteed, automatic increase that you cannot get anywhere else. If your full retirement age benefit is three thousand dollars a month, waiting just two years takes that to around three thousand four hundred and eighty dollars. Permanently. Every month for the rest of your life, and depending on your situation, for your spouse after you're gone.

But here's where people go wrong. Waiting is not always the right answer. If you have a health condition that shortens your life expectancy, the break-even math changes completely. If you and your spouse have very different ages or benefit amounts, the spousal benefit strategy becomes critical. And if you retire at 62 and have no other income coming in, waiting to claim might mean selling investments at the worst possible time just to cover your living expenses.

The point is not that you should claim early or late. The point is that this is a permanent, irrevocable decision that deserves more than a quick calculation on a napkin. Run the actual numbers. Model your specific situation. This one decision can be worth tens of thousands of dollars over your lifetime. Treat it that way.

Decision two. How much to convert to a Roth before your paycheck stops.

I want to reframe the way you think about this. Most people think about Roth conversions as something you do when you're young. But here's what nobody tells you: the years right before and right after you retire are often the lowest-income years you will have had since your twenties. Your salary stops, but your Required Minimum Distributions haven't started yet. Your bracket drops. And for the first time in decades, you have room to move money into a Roth at a rate you probably could not have touched during your career.

If you have a traditional IRA or a 401k, that money has never been taxed. Every dollar that comes out in retirement gets taxed at ordinary income rates. And here's what that means in practice: if your RMDs at 73 push you into the 22 or 24 percent bracket, you will pay that rate on money you could have converted at 10 or 12 percent during your transition years.

The Roth conversion window is not a tax trick. It's arithmetic. The money gets taxed either way. The question is when and at what rate. For the right household in the right years, this decision is worth real dollars. Do the math now, before the window closes.

Decision three. How you are going to bridge healthcare before Medicare.

This one catches more people off guard than almost anything else in retirement planning, and it is completely avoidable if you think about it in advance.

Medicare starts at 65. Not at 62. Not at 64 and a half. At 65. If you retire before that, you have a gap, and that gap has to be covered by something. COBRA lets you extend your employer's coverage, but it is expensive because you are now paying both your share and your employer's share of the premium. The marketplace is another option, but the cost depends heavily on your income and what subsidies you qualify for. A spouse's plan, if one is available, can be the most cost-effective path.

None of these options are automatically bad. But each one has different costs, different implications for how you structure your income in those years, and different trade-offs. The mistake is assuming you'll figure it out when you get there. People who figure it out when they get there typically pay more than people who planned for it twelve months earlier. Budget this number into your retirement income plan before you give notice.

Decision four. Building your income floor.

Here is a concept that changes the way people think about retirement. Your income floor is the total amount of guaranteed monthly income you need to cover your non-negotiable expenses. Housing, food, utilities, insurance. The basics. Everything that has to be paid regardless of what the market does.

Your Social Security covers part of that floor. A pension covers part of it if you have one. For many people, there is a gap between their guaranteed income and what they need every month. That gap is what you have to close before you retire, and you have options. An annuity can fill it. A bond ladder can fill it. A systematic withdrawal strategy can fill it. Which one makes sense depends on your situation, your risk tolerance, and your other assets.

But the principle is not negotiable. You want guaranteed income covering your fixed costs before you touch your investment portfolio. Because if you do not have that floor in place, every market downturn becomes an emergency instead of an inconvenience. That is not a retirement. That is a second career managing stress.

Decision five. When in the year you actually retire.

This is the one almost nobody talks about, and it might be the one that surprises you most.

The sequence of returns risk, meaning the risk that the market drops early in your retirement, is most dangerous in your first two years. If you retire in October and the market falls fifteen percent by December, and you are pulling from your portfolio to cover living expenses, you are selling shares at the worst possible price. Those shares are gone, and so is their future growth. You cannot get that back.

This means the calendar actually matters. When you retire within the year, what your cash reserves look like on day one, and how many months of expenses you can cover without touching your investments, these are not details you figure out later. They are part of your retirement plan. Walking into retirement with two years of expenses in cash or short-term reserves is not being overly conservative. It is giving your portfolio time to recover if it needs to.

Decision six. Whether you have a written plan.

I saved this one for last because it is the one that ties everything else together.

Most people I talk to who are twelve to eighteen months from retirement have a general idea. They know roughly what Social Security will pay them. They know roughly what's in their accounts. They have a date in mind. And they feel like they have a handle on it.

A general idea is not a plan. A general idea is what gets you to the first year of retirement and then realizes you have not thought through what happens when the market drops and your RMDs haven't started and your healthcare bridge is more expensive than you expected and Social Security turned out to be less than you thought.

A real plan has all six of these decisions documented, modeled, and connected to each other. It has a Social Security strategy. It has a Roth conversion strategy for the transition years. It has a healthcare bridge. It has an income floor. It has a sequence buffer. And it gives you a document you can actually look at when things get uncertain, that reminds you what you decided and why you decided it.

That is what financial planning in the last year before retirement actually looks like. Not a meeting once a year to review your allocation. A real plan, built for this specific moment in your life.

Proverbs 21:5 says the plans of the diligent lead to profit. This is what that looks like in practice. Not rushing. Not guessing. Not defaulting to whatever feels safest in the moment. Sitting down, running the actual numbers on each of these six decisions, and making them on purpose before the window closes.
