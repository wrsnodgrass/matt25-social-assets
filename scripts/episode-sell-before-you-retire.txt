There is a version of retirement where you have more money, less stress, and more freedom on day one than you ever thought possible. And there is a version where you drag the same financial complexity you had during your working years right into retirement with you, and spend the next decade trying to untangle it.

The difference is often what you chose to sell before you stopped working.

Today I am going to walk you through five things you should seriously consider selling before you retire, and more importantly, what to do with the money when you do. Because selling the right things at the right time is not just about simplifying your life. It is one of the most effective ways to fund the retirement you actually want.

Before I get into it: I am going to use round numbers and hypothetical examples to make this concrete. Your situation is different. Talk to your own advisor before you act on any of this.

Number one. The extra vehicle.

Most households that are working have two vehicles because they need two vehicles. Two people commuting, two different schedules, two sets of obligations pulling in different directions every day. When you retire, that math usually changes. One person is no longer commuting. Errands and appointments become something you can coordinate. For many couples, the transition from two cars to one car is not a sacrifice. It is a relief.

Here is what that actually looks like financially. A paid-off vehicle sitting in your garage is still costing you money every month. Insurance. Registration. Maintenance. Tires. Depreciation. When you add it up over the first ten years of retirement, an extra vehicle you rarely use can easily cost you thirty to fifty thousand dollars when you factor in what that capital could have done sitting in your retirement account instead.

Sell it before you retire while your income is still high and the decision feels low-stakes. Use the proceeds to build your cash buffer, the reserves you want on hand in the first two years of retirement so you are not forced to sell investments when the market is down. This is a small asset that solves a specific and important problem.

Number two. The house you have outgrown.

I know this one is harder. But let me make the case for it, and then you can decide.

Most people wait until after they retire to downsize. The kids are gone, the house is paid off or close to it, and they figure they will get around to it eventually. But waiting until after you retire has real costs that most people do not see coming.

When you are still working, you have income to qualify for a mortgage on the new place if you need one. Your debt-to-income ratio is favorable. If you sell the family home while you are still earning, you have more flexibility in how you structure the purchase of your next home. Once you retire and your income drops, qualifying for a mortgage becomes significantly harder even if your net worth is substantial.

There is also the tax picture. If you have lived in your home for two of the last five years, the first five hundred thousand in gains is excluded from federal capital gains tax for a married couple. If your home has appreciated well past that threshold, the strategy around timing and how you take the proceeds matters. This is exactly the kind of decision that benefits from being made before retirement, not during it.

The equity in your home is often the single largest asset a pre-retiree has outside of their retirement accounts. Unlocking that equity at the right time, into the right structure, can fund your income floor, your Roth conversion strategy, and your first few years of retirement more effectively than almost any other move you can make.

Number three. The life insurance death benefit you have outgrown.

This one surprises people.

Permanent life insurance is sold heavily during the accumulation years, and for good reason. When you have young children, a mortgage, and a spouse who depends on your income, a large death benefit makes sense. It protects against the financial catastrophe of dying too soon.

But your needs change. By the time you are approaching retirement, your kids are likely grown and financially independent. Your mortgage may be paid off. Your spouse has their own Social Security benefit coming. The question you need to ask is: what is this death benefit actually protecting at this point in my life, and is the premium I am paying for it the best use of that money?

In many cases, permanent life insurance policies have accumulated substantial cash value. That cash value is your money. And there are options: surrender the policy, do a life settlement, convert it to a paid-up policy. Which path makes sense depends on the specifics of your policy and your situation. But the point is this: if you are paying for a death benefit that no longer matches your actual risk, you have an asset that is underperforming its potential. Getting that right before retirement is worth the conversation.

Number four. The vacation property or second home.

Second homes and vacation properties are one of the most common ways pre-retirees accidentally complicate their retirement.

During your working years, the vacation home feels like a reward. A destination. Something to look forward to. But when you retire and time is no longer the scarce resource, the picture shifts. You are not just using the property a few weekends a year anymore. You are now responsible for maintaining it indefinitely, managing it if it is a rental, paying taxes and insurance on it, and potentially watching it anchor you to one location when what you actually want in retirement is the freedom to go anywhere.

Properties that made perfect sense during your working years can become liabilities in retirement. And properties sold before retirement, especially in a strong real estate market, can unlock significant capital at exactly the moment you need it most. The proceeds can fund your income floor, reduce the amount you need to draw from your investment portfolio in the early years, and give you the flexibility to rent in places you love rather than owning in one.

Number five. The stock concentration you have been quietly ignoring.

This is the one most people know they should address and keep putting off.

If you have been with an employer for twenty or thirty years, if you have exercised stock options, participated in an ESOP, or simply accumulated company stock in your 401k, there is a real chance that one company represents a disproportionate percentage of your net worth. And as long as the stock kept going up, there was never a compelling reason to do anything about it.

Retirement changes the calculation completely. In accumulation, concentration is a risk you can absorb because you have time and income to recover from a bad outcome. In distribution, concentration is a risk that can permanently damage your plan. If a single company represents twenty or thirty percent of your portfolio and it cuts its dividend, misses earnings, or faces a regulatory problem, the impact on your retirement income is not theoretical.

Reducing that concentration before you retire, spread across a few tax years if the position is large, keeps you in control of the timing and the tax rate. Waiting until after retirement, when your income picture is less predictable and your ability to absorb a loss is lower, means the market decides for you instead.

Here is the common thread in all five of these. In every case, selling before you retire gives you more control than waiting. Control over timing. Control over tax treatment. Control over how the proceeds get deployed. Once you are retired, your options narrow and the cost of getting it wrong goes up.

Proverbs 13:11 says wealth gained hastily will dwindle, but whoever gathers little by little will increase it. Good stewardship is not about holding on to everything you have accumulated. It is about being intentional with each asset you carry into the next season of life. The things you sell on purpose, at the right time, for the right reason, fund the freedom you have been working toward.
